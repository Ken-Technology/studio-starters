Five moves for operations leaders at multi-site dental groups

Groups that grow by acquisition end up with the same problem: the chairs are there, the patients are there, and the schedule still leaks. This guide covers five moves that fill those chairs without adding staff or software. Each one comes with an example and something you can start this week.

The five moves

  1. Book the next visit before the patient leaves
  2. Keep one recall list across every system
  3. Fill cancellations from a short list
  4. Measure chair hours, not appointment counts
  5. Give every regional manager the same Monday page

01of five

Book the next visit before the patient leaves

Recall cards and reminder texts chase patients who have already gone home. Checkout is the one moment every patient stands in front of you with a calendar in their pocket, and it is the moment most practices spend on payment alone. The booking that happens there never needs chasing.

Example

A pediatric practice in the group makes checkout a two-step script. The assistant walks the parent to the desk and names the month of the next visit before handing over. The coordinator offers two times, books one, and only then asks about payment. The parent leaves with the appointment on their phone, and that family drops off the recall list for good.

First step

Pick one practice and add one line to the checkout script: name the month of the next visit before the patient pays. Count for a week how many leave with a booking.

02of five

Keep one recall list across every system

Three practice management systems mean three definitions of overdue and three teams pulling lists on different days. Patients at the newest practices fall through first, because their records were migrated last and nobody owns them yet. One list with one definition is the first thing a regional manager can act on.

Example

The regional manager for Portland exports the overdue list from each of her practices on Monday morning, pastes them into one sheet with the same four columns, and splits the calls by practice. On Friday she marks who booked. The sheet is ugly, but it is the first time the Spokane practices appear on the same page as the rest of the group.

First step

Ask every practice for its overdue list in whatever shape it comes, and paste them into one sheet with the same columns. Do not clean the data yet.

03of five

Fill cancellations from a short list

A same-day cancellation is worth nothing unless someone fills it within the hour, and nobody can call the whole recall list in an hour. The practices that fill their gaps keep a short list of patients who asked to come sooner, and they text that list before they do anything else.

Example

A hygienist keeps a note on the schedule screen: patients who said they would take an earlier slot if one opened. When a Tuesday afternoon cancels, the coordinator texts those patients first, in order, and the chair is filled before lunch. Nobody opens the full recall list, and the patient who moved up thanks the front desk on the way out.

First step

Add a column to the schedule notes for patients who asked to be called sooner, and tell the coordinator to text that list first when a slot opens.

04of five

Measure chair hours, not appointment counts

Appointment counts hide the gaps. A day can look full and still have an hour of empty chair between procedures, and the practice management reports will never show it because they count visits, not minutes. Chair hours tell you what each practice could have produced and where the leak is.

Example

An office manager marks the schedule by hand for one week: every block of empty chair, with the reason next to it. Late arrival, no show, a procedure that ran short, a hygienist out. At the end of the week the reasons sort themselves into three piles, and two of them are things the front desk can change.

First step

Ask one office manager to mark empty chair time and its reason on a printed schedule for one week, then read the reasons together.

05of five

Give every regional manager the same Monday page

Regional managers hired this year inherit practices with different habits and no shared view, so each one builds a report of their own and the numbers never match. One page per practice, in the same shape, turns thirty one stories into one meeting and makes the weak practice visible without blame.

Example

The operations lead drafts the page by hand for one region: booked at checkout, cancellations filled, overdue patients called, chair hours lost. It goes out on Monday to the regional manager and every office manager in that region. The first version is wrong in two places, which is how everyone learns what the numbers mean.

First step

Write the Monday page for one region in a plain document with four lines per practice. Send it, and ask each office manager what is missing.

Where to start

Start with one move this week

Start with the first move. It costs nothing, the front desk can run it tomorrow, and every patient booked at checkout is one fewer to chase later. The other four build on it in order: the list gets shorter, the gaps get filled, the measure gets honest, and the Monday page shows all of it.
